Understanding the Operations Role

Develop a foundational understanding of Operations, the responsibilities of Operations professionals, and the value Operations brings to a real estate organization.

What Operations is, and why it matters

This module introduces the Operations career path and establishes how Operations supports business success.

1

What are business operations?

Business Operations encompass the people, processes, systems, and daily activities that keep an organization running efficiently. They provide the structure needed to coordinate work, support collaboration, and ensure business objectives are achieved consistently. Rather than focusing on one department, Operations connects every area of the business, creating alignment between teams, workflows, and organizational goals.

PeopleWho does the work
ProcessesHow the work gets done
SystemsThe tools that hold it together
2

Why Operations matter

Operations provide the foundation that allows a business to grow without losing efficiency or consistency. Well-managed operations reduce bottlenecks, improve communication, standardize processes, and help teams work more effectively. As organizations grow, strong operations become essential for maintaining quality, supporting collaboration, and delivering a consistent client experience.

3

The purpose of Operations

The purpose of Operations is to create an organized, efficient, and scalable business environment. Operations establishes clear processes, supports collaboration between departments, and ensures resources are used effectively. By creating structure and improving workflows, Operations enables the business to achieve its objectives while adapting to growth and change.

4

How Operations supports business success

Operations supports business success by ensuring that people, processes, and systems work together efficiently. It improves visibility across the organization, promotes accountability, reduces operational risk, and creates consistency in daily activities.

In a real estate business

Strong operations allow teams to manage transactions, clients, marketing, and administrative functions in a coordinated and reliable manner.

The Operations career path

Operations offers a progressive career path where responsibilities evolve from executing tasks to managing processes, leading teams, and driving business strategy. As professionals advance, their focus shifts from supporting daily operations to improving systems, optimizing performance, and contributing to organizational growth.

  1. Operations Assistant

    Supports day-to-day administrative and operational tasks by following established processes and assisting different departments. This role focuses on execution, organization, and learning business operations.

  2. Operations Specialist

    Takes ownership of specific operational processes or business functions. In addition to executing tasks, this role identifies opportunities for improvement, ensures process consistency, and serves as a subject matter expert within their area of responsibility.

  3. Junior Operations Manager

    Begins coordinating multiple operational processes while supporting leadership with planning, reporting, process improvement, and cross-functional collaboration. This role bridges execution and operational management.

  4. Operations Manager

    Oversees business operations by coordinating people, processes, and systems to ensure efficiency, consistency, and alignment with business objectives. Operations Managers analyze performance, solve operational challenges, and lead continuous improvement initiatives.

  5. Director of Operations

    Provides strategic leadership for the organization's operations. This role focuses on operational strategy, organizational performance, scalability, resource planning, and long-term business growth while supporting leadership in achieving company objectives.

The shift to notice

Each step moves the focus further from doing the task and closer to improving the system that produces it: execution, then ownership, then coordination, then improvement, then strategy.

Typical responsibilities

Operations professionals commonly support process coordination, project and task management, documentation, reporting, cross-functional communication, vendor and client support, performance tracking, and administrative oversight to ensure daily operations run smoothly.

Scope of ownership

As Operations professionals advance in their careers, their responsibilities expand from executing assigned tasks to owning processes, managing projects, supporting teams, and driving operational improvements that align with business objectives.

Operational coordination

Operations acts as the central point of coordination between departments, helping ensure that information, resources, priorities, and workflows remain aligned while minimizing delays and communication gaps.

Process improvement responsibilities

A key responsibility of Operations is identifying inefficiencies, standardizing workflows, documenting processes, and recommending improvements that increase consistency, productivity, and scalability.

Example: Real Estate Operations Manager responsibilities

The Real Estate Operations Manager is responsible for overseeing the systems, processes, people, and administrative functions that support the daily operations of a residential real estate organization.

This role serves as the operational backbone of the business, ensuring that transactions, listings, marketing activities, lead management, internal systems, documentation, and team workflows are organized, accurate, efficient, and completed according to established timelines.

The Operations Manager is expected to identify operational gaps, improve processes, maintain accountability across departments, and provide management with clear visibility into ongoing projects and business priorities.

  • Maintain organized workflows across listings, transactions, marketing, lead management, referrals, administration, and internal projects.
  • Monitor operational timelines, deadlines, recurring responsibilities, and deliverables.
  • Follow up with team members to ensure assignments are completed accurately and on time.
  • Identify operational bottlenecks, inefficiencies, and opportunities for process improvement.
  • Maintain project management systems and operational tracking tools.
  • Support management with quarterly and annual operational planning.

  • Work with department owners to document new or changing processes.
  • Support the implementation and adoption of updated workflows.

Company foundations

Mission, vision, core values and culture explain why a company works the way it does. An Operations professional reads them first, because every process should serve them.

1

Mission

The mission states why the company exists today: who it serves, what it does for them and how. It guides daily decisions and should be short enough to remember.

How to write it

  1. Name who the company serves.
  2. Describe what it does for them.
  3. Say how it does it differently.
  4. Cut it to one or two sentences.

Example

Help growing real estate teams run organized, stress-free operations so agents can focus on their clients.

2

Vision

The vision describes where the company wants to be in the future. It gives direction to long-term planning and tells the team what all the daily work is building toward.

How to write it

  1. Picture the company in five to ten years.
  2. Describe the impact it will have.
  3. Keep it ambitious but believable.

Mission versus vision

The mission is today's purpose; the vision is tomorrow's destination. Operations translates both into processes, priorities and KPIs.

3

Core Values define the behaviors and principles that guide decision-making and company culture.

How to create them

  1. Identify the behaviors the company wants to encourage.
  2. Select 4 to 6 values that reflect the organization's culture.
  3. Define what each value means in practice.
  4. Reinforce them through leadership and daily operations.

Understanding existing operations

Before introducing changes, Operations professionals must first understand how a business currently operates. Every company has its own workflows, priorities, challenges, and ways of working.

Taking the time to learn existing operations helps build trust, reduce unnecessary disruption, and ensure that improvements address real business needs rather than assumptions.

1

Why businesses create SOPs

Purpose

Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) document how recurring tasks and processes should be performed to ensure consistency, quality, and efficiency across the organization.

Why they matter

SOPs help businesses standardize work, reduce errors, improve onboarding, and ensure critical knowledge is not dependent on a single individual. They also provide a consistent reference for employees when performing operational tasks.

  • Keep SOPs clear, accurate, and easy to follow.
  • Review and update them regularly.
  • Ensure they reflect how work is actually performed.
  • Use SOPs as a guide while remaining open to continuous improvement.

Want to go deeper? The SOP Foundations training in the VA path teaches how to write one step by step.

2

Why every company operates differently

Every business develops its operations based on its goals, clients, team structure, technology, and leadership style. As a result, two companies performing similar services may have very different workflows, responsibilities, approval processes, or communication methods.

Operations professionals should avoid assuming that one company's processes are automatically better than another's. The objective is to understand why a process exists before determining whether it should be improved.

  • Stay curious and avoid making assumptions.
  • Learn the business before comparing it to previous experiences.
  • Respect existing processes while evaluating opportunities for improvement.
  • Adapt to the client's operational model rather than expecting it to match another organization.
3

Understanding before changing

One of the most important responsibilities of an Operations professional is to understand the current operation before recommending changes. Effective improvements are based on observation, analysis, and business objectives, not personal preference.

Before proposing any improvement, ask
  • What problem is this process solving?
  • Why was it designed this way?
  • Is the process achieving its intended outcome?
  • Who is affected by this process?
  • What risks could result from changing it?

Only after understanding the current state should recommendations be made.

  • Observe before acting.
  • Gather information from the people involved.
  • Validate assumptions with data whenever possible.
  • Recommend improvements that support business goals rather than adding unnecessary complexity.

Systems thinking and business processes

Systems Thinking is the ability to view a business as a collection of interconnected processes, people, and systems rather than isolated tasks or departments.

1

Systems thinking

Every action within an organization influences other areas, making it essential to understand how work flows across the business.

For an Operations professional, Systems Thinking means looking beyond immediate problems to understand their root causes and overall business impact. Instead of optimizing one task, the goal is to improve the entire system while maintaining alignment with business objectives.

  • Businesses operate through interconnected systems.
  • Changes in one process often affect multiple departments.
  • Operations professionals focus on improving the system, not individual tasks.
2

Business processes

A business process is a sequence of structured activities performed to achieve a specific business objective. Every recurring task within an organization should belong to a defined process that produces a consistent outcome.

Well-designed business processes improve efficiency, reduce errors, simplify training, and create operational consistency.

Operations Framework

How to create a business process

  1. Define the objectiveClearly identify what the process is intended to accomplish.
  2. Identify the process ownerDetermine who is responsible for managing and maintaining the process.
  3. List the major activitiesIdentify the key tasks required to complete the process.
  4. Define inputs and outputsDocument what starts the process and the expected final deliverable.
  5. Document the processOrganize the sequence of activities in a clear and consistent format.
  6. Validate with stakeholdersReview the process with the people involved to ensure accuracy and completeness.
  7. Review and improveContinuously evaluate the process and update it as the business evolves.

A well-designed business process creates consistency, improves efficiency, reduces errors, and provides a foundation for continuous improvement.

  • Focus on outcomes, not individual tasks.
  • Keep processes simple and repeatable.
  • Review processes regularly for improvement opportunities.

Workflows, inputs, outputs and dependencies

A process says what gets done. A workflow shows how the work moves, what it needs to start, what it produces, and what has to happen first.

1

Operational workflows

An operational workflow illustrates how work moves from one step, person, or department to another. It helps teams understand responsibilities, approval points, dependencies, and the overall flow of work.

How to create an operational workflow

  1. Define the starting point.
  2. Identify each activity in sequence.
  3. Assign ownership for every step.
  4. Document handoff points between departments.
  5. Identify decision or approval stages.
  6. Define the expected outcome.
  7. Validate the workflow with the people involved.

Best practices

  • Keep workflows easy to follow.

  • Update workflows whenever processes change.

Process dependencies

Process dependencies are relationships between activities where one task cannot begin until another has been completed. Recognizing these dependencies helps Operations professionals coordinate work, reduce delays, and improve planning.   6. Team training begins.

Since each step depends on the completion of the previous one, skipping or delaying any task can impact the entire onboarding process. Understanding these dependencies helps Operations professionals coordinate work effectively, minimize delays, and ensure a smooth client transition.

Your team has spent two quarters incrementally speeding up the manual document-review process, each improvement shaves a few minutes off. You've just learned an automation tool could replace the manual review almost entirely.

1

The concept

There's a real difference between making an existing process modestly better and asking whether the process should exist in its current form at all. Incremental improvement is valuable and often the right call, but it's worth periodically stepping back and asking whether you're optimizing something that should instead be redesigned or replaced.

  • What is the principle? Distinguish between improving a process and questioning whether it's still the right process.
  • Why does it matter? Teams can spend real effort perfecting a process that a different approach would make obsolete.
  • Ineffective behavior: continuing incremental optimization without periodically asking if a bigger shift is available.
  • Effective behavior: evaluating the automation option honestly against the cost of continuing incremental work, even if it means the past two quarters' effort gets partially superseded.
  • Common mistake: sunk-cost thinking, continuing down the incremental path because of the effort already invested in it.
Example

Two quarters of shaving minutes off manual review might add up to real savings, but if automation removes the review step almost entirely, the higher-impact move is evaluating that, not defending the incremental work already done.

2

Think it through

  • What's the total time saved by the incremental improvements so far, and what's the ceiling on that approach?
  • What would automation actually replace, and what would it cost to implement and validate?
  • Is the reluctance to explore automation based on real constraints, or on the effort already invested incrementally?
  • What's the honest comparison between continuing to optimize and switching approaches?

Keeping Operations Aligned to Values

Skills: Strategic Thinking and Organization

A shortcut in the client-onboarding process would save real time, but it skips a step where clients are explicitly asked about their expectations, which the company's stated values describe as central to its client relationships.

1

The concept

Enduring organizations stay aligned to their core values even as specific practices evolve, values act as a filter for which efficiencies are actually worth taking. A shortcut that's efficient on paper but quietly erodes something the business says it stands for isn't a neutral trade-off; it's a real cost, even if it doesn't show up in a time-saved metric.

  • Why does it matter? Efficiency gains that erode stated values compound into a gap between what the company says it is and how it actually operates.

  • Common mistake: assuming values are aspirational language that doesn't need to inform operational decisions.

Skipping the expectations conversation might save fifteen minutes per onboarding, but if the company's values explicitly center understanding the client, that fifteen minutes may be exactly the wrong place to cut.

Turning Strategy Into a Weekly Rhythm

Skills: Execution and Organization

Leadership set three clear priorities for the quarter two months ago. Looking at the team's actual weekly work, none of the three has visibly moved, everyone's still doing what they were doing before the priorities were set.

1

The concept

A strategy that doesn't show up in the weekly rhythm of the business isn't really operating, it's a document. Turning priorities into execution requires a consistent operating cadence: a recurring meeting that reviews progress, surfaces blockers, and keeps the stated priorities visibly connected to this week's actual work.

  • What is the principle? Strategy becomes real through a consistent rhythm, a recurring check-in that connects stated priorities to actual weekly work, not through a one-time announcement.
  • Why does it matter? Without a rhythm, quarterly priorities quietly lose to whatever's urgent that day, every day, until the quarter ends with no visible movement.
  • Ineffective behavior: setting priorities once and assuming the team will self-organize around them.
  • Effective behavior: a short, recurring meeting that explicitly reviews progress against the stated priorities and surfaces what's blocking them.
  • Common mistake: confusing being busy with making progress on what was actually prioritized.

A 20-minute weekly meeting that starts with "where are we on the three quarterly priorities" keeps them visible in a way a one-time kickoff announcement can't.

Creating Real Accountability

Skills: Execution and Decision-Making

A team member has missed the same weekly deadline three times. Each time, they apologize sincerely and say it won't happen again, and each time, it does.

1

The concept

Real accountability is different from a sincere apology, it requires a specific commitment, a way to track it, and a real consequence if it's missed again. Without those three things, 'it won't happen again' is a statement of intent, not a mechanism that actually changes behavior.

  • What is the principle? Accountability requires a specific commitment, a visible way to track it, and a genuine consequence, not just good intentions.
  • Why does it matter? Repeated sincere apologies without a changed mechanism train the team that missed deadlines have no real cost.
  • Ineffective behavior: accepting the apology and moving on with the same vague expectation as before.
  • Effective behavior: naming the specific pattern, agreeing on a concrete commitment, and defining what happens if it's missed a fourth time.
  • Common mistake: mistaking a sincere apology for a solved problem.
Example

"I'll try to be better about it" isn't a commitment. "I'll send you the draft by 3pm each Thursday, and if I'm going to miss it, I'll flag it by noon" is, it's specific and trackable.

2

Think it through

  • What's different this time that would make a fourth commitment more likely to hold than the first three?
  • What specific, trackable commitment could replace a general intention to do better?
  • What real consequence, communicated clearly in advance, would make this commitment meaningful?
  • Is there a root cause behind the pattern that hasn't been addressed yet?

Building Trust Before Expecting Healthy Conflict

Skills: People Management and Stakeholder Management

In team meetings, disagreements rarely surface out loud, people nod along, then you hear the real objections afterward, one-on-one, in the hallway.

1

The concept

Teams that avoid open disagreement usually aren't avoiding conflict itself, they're avoiding it because trust hasn't been established enough for disagreement to feel safe. Without trust, healthy conflict, real commitment, and mutual accountability all become difficult, because everyone is protecting themselves rather than working through the actual disagreement together.

  • What is the principle? Trust is the foundation that makes open, healthy conflict possible, without it, disagreement goes underground instead of getting resolved.
  • Why does it matter? A team that avoids visible conflict often isn't more aligned, it's just less honest, and the real disagreements resurface later as quiet resistance.
  • Ineffective behavior: treating a lack of visible disagreement as a sign of alignment.
  • Effective behavior: actively inviting disagreement in the room, and modeling that raising concerns has no downside.
  • Common mistake: assuming trust exists because no one has said otherwise.
Example

Explicitly asking "what's the strongest objection to this plan?" in a meeting, and genuinely welcoming the answer, does more to build trust than any number of team-building exercises.

2

Think it through

  • What might be making the team feel disagreement isn't safe to raise directly?
  • What have you modeled, intentionally or not, about how disagreement gets received?
  • What would it look like to actively invite the objections that usually surface afterward?
  • What's the cost of hallway disagreements versus disagreements raised and resolved in the room?

Balancing Short-Term Pressure and Long-Term Priorities

Skills: Strategic Thinking and Decision-Making

A capstone scenario: leadership wants a quick win to show the board next month. Your team's real priority, a multi-quarter process overhaul that several other lessons in this path have touched on, is only 40% complete and would visibly stall if you diverted attention now.

1

The concept

This is a synthesis lesson: every principle in this path, discovery before recommending, deciding under pressure, influence, trust, values alignment, and real accountability, shows up in some form when short-term pressure threatens a long-term priority. The skill isn't picking one over the other by default; it's finding the option that protects the long-term work while genuinely addressing the short-term need.

  • What is the principle? Short-term pressure and long-term priorities aren't automatically in conflict, but treating every urgent request as equally important to your core priority erodes the long-term work by a thousand small diversions.
  • Why does it matter? An Operations Manager who always defers to whatever's urgent this week never actually delivers the multi-quarter work that produces the biggest business impact.
  • Ineffective behavior: dropping the long-term priority entirely for the visible short-term win, or refusing the short-term ask outright.
  • Effective behavior: negotiating a version of the short-term ask that protects the long-term work, communicated honestly to leadership.
  • Common mistake: treating this as a binary choice instead of a negotiation with real options on both sides.
Example

A smaller, scoped 'quick win', a visible metric from the overhaul's first 40%, rather than a brand-new initiative, might satisfy the board update without pulling the team off the real priority.

  • What's already true from the 40%-complete overhaul that could be framed as a genuine, honest win?
  • What would fully diverting the team cost the long-term priority, in concrete terms?
  • How do you negotiate this honestly, using what you've practiced across this path, discovery, influence, and a clear trade-off conversation?
