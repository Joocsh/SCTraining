Think like an Operations Manager

From Task Executor to Systems Thinker

Two members of the transaction team keep re-doing the same fix: listing data pulled into the CRM from the intake form is missing unit numbers about once a week. Each time it happens, whoever notices it manually corrects the record and moves on. It's never taken more than ten minutes, so no one has flagged it as a problem.

1

The concept

Business Operations connect every part of the company, people, process, and systems, rather than living inside one task. Systems Thinking means looking at a business as a set of interconnected processes instead of isolated jobs, and asking why a problem keeps happening rather than just fixing it again.

  • A task fixed twice is a symptom. A task fixed every week is a process problem.
  • Every action in an organization influences other areas, a small data gap upstream becomes a reporting or client-trust problem downstream.
  • The goal is not to optimize one task faster; it's to improve the system so the task stops recurring.
Example

If the intake form itself doesn't require a unit number, every downstream fix is temporary. The real fix is upstream, at the input, not at the person doing the correction.

2

Think it through

  • What is the real problem, the missing unit numbers, or something upstream from them?
  • Who is quietly absorbing the cost of this recurring fix, and is anyone tracking that cost?
  • What assumption is the team making by treating this as 'no big deal'?
  • What would happen if this kept recurring for another six months unaddressed?

Understanding Before Changing

You've just joined a brokerage as their new Operations Manager. In your first week, you notice the client onboarding process has seven steps and involves three different people signing off. It looks inefficient compared to a system you used at your last company.

1

The concept

Every business builds its operations around its own goals, clients, team, and history. A process that looks inefficient from the outside may exist for a reason that isn't visible yet. Effective improvement starts with understanding the current state, not replacing it with what worked somewhere else.

  • Ask what problem a process solves and why it was designed this way before judging it.
  • Two companies doing similar work can have very different, and equally valid, workflows.
  • Changing a process without understanding it first can damage trust and create new risk you didn't anticipate.
Example

The three sign-offs might exist because a past deal fell through when a step was skipped. Removing it without knowing that history could reintroduce the exact risk it was built to prevent.

2

Think it through

  • What problem might this process have originally been designed to solve?
  • Who are the three people signing off, and what would break if one were removed?
  • What data or history do you not yet have access to?
  • What's the risk of changing this in week one versus week six?

Asking Better Questions

A department head tells you, "the handoff between sales and onboarding is broken." That's the entire brief you're given. No specifics, no examples, no data.

1

The concept

Operational Discovery starts with asking open-ended questions that clarify processes, responsibilities, challenges, and expectations, good questions often reveal opportunities that aren't immediately visible. A vague complaint is a starting point, not a diagnosis.

  • Ask what's happening, not just what's wrong, "walk me through the last time this happened" surfaces more than "what's broken?"
  • Open-ended questions (what, how, walk me through) get description; closed questions (is it X?) get agreement or denial, which teaches you less.
  • The person reporting a problem is describing their experience of it, not necessarily its cause, treat their framing as a lead, not a conclusion.
Example

"Is it the CRM's fault?" invites a yes/no. "Walk me through what happens from the moment a deal closes to when onboarding starts" surfaces the actual sequence, and usually the actual gap.

2

Think it through

  • What does 'broken' actually mean to this department head, slow, inconsistent, or missing entirely?
  • Whose perspective are you missing, have you heard from anyone on the onboarding side yet?
  • What's the difference between what they're describing and what they're assuming is the cause?
  • What would a specific, walk-me-through example reveal that a general complaint can't?

Root Problems vs. Symptoms

Client complaints about slow response times have tripled this quarter. Leadership's instinct is to hire another support coordinator to handle the volume.

1

The concept

A symptom is what you can see (slow responses). A root cause is what's producing it (volume, a broken triage step, an unclear ownership rule, or a tool that makes replying harder than it should be). Adding headcount treats the symptom and can mask, or even entrench, the real cause.

  • Before adding resources, ask whether the volume itself grew, or whether the same volume is simply taking longer to handle than it used to.
  • 'More people' is often the most expensive fix available and the easiest to reach for under pressure, cheaper root-cause fixes are worth ruling out first.
  • If the root cause is a process gap, adding headcount without fixing it usually means you'll need to add headcount again next quarter.
Example

If ticket volume is flat but response time tripled, the cause isn't volume, something changed in the process itself (a tool switch, an unclear routing rule, a coordinator being pulled onto other work).

2

Think it through

  • Has ticket volume actually increased, or has response time increased at the same volume?
  • What changed in the last quarter, tools, staffing, priorities, around the time this started?
  • If you hired someone tomorrow, would the same bottleneck just absorb one more person?
  • What would you need to look at to tell volume and process apart?

Mapping Business Impact & Dependencies

Marketing wants to launch a new lead-capture form next week. On the surface it's a small change, one form, one page. You've been asked to sign off.

1

The concept

Every process has dependencies, relationships where one task can't begin until another finishes. A small-looking change at one point in the system can ripple into CRM fields, follow-up automations, and reporting that nobody thought to check before launch.

  • Map what touches this change before approving it: what systems read from this form, what automations trigger off it, what reports depend on its fields.
  • A change that looks isolated from where it's requested (marketing) may not be isolated from where it lands (CRM, sales follow-up, reporting).
  • Five minutes mapping dependencies is cheaper than a week of broken automations discovered after launch.
Example

If the new form doesn't map to the same CRM fields as the old one, every automated follow-up email tied to those fields silently stops firing, and nobody notices until a lead goes cold.

2

Think it through

  • What systems or automations currently depend on the existing form's fields?
  • Who would be the first to notice if something downstream broke, and would they know to tell you?
  • What's the cost of checking dependencies now versus discovering a break after launch?
  • Is this actually a small change, or does it only look small from where the request originated?

Operational Awareness in Practice

You're sitting in on a leadership meeting as an observer. The CEO mentions client retention has dipped slightly this quarter, then moves on to the next topic without much discussion.

1

The concept

Operational Awareness means noticing what a business impact actually implies, even when no one in the room flags it as urgent. It's the habit of connecting a small, easily-overlooked comment to the operational systems that might be behind it.

  • A comment that gets one sentence in a meeting isn't necessarily low-priority, it may just be unowned.
  • Operations professionals build credibility by connecting business signals (a retention dip) to operational causes (onboarding quality, response times, follow-up consistency) that others in the room aren't positioned to see.
  • Awareness without follow-through is just noticing, the value comes from bringing a specific next step, not just flagging that something seems off.
Example

A slight retention dip might trace back to onboarding delays, inconsistent follow-up, or a support bottleneck, operational threads the CEO isn't positioned to pull on, but you are.

2

Think it through

  • Why might this have gotten only a sentence of attention, is it actually low-priority, or just unowned?
  • What would it look like to investigate this without overstepping or alarming anyone prematurely?
  • What's the cost of letting this pass unaddressed for another quarter?

Giving Feedback That Builds Trust

Your Transaction Coordinator has missed the document-review deadline three times this month. Each time, the client's closing timeline slipped by a day. They haven't mentioned any blockers, and other teammates have started quietly picking up the slack.

1

The concept

Accountability means owning decisions, actions, and results while staying reliable and transparent, and giving feedback is how an Operations Manager protects both accountability and trust at the same time. Feedback that's delayed, vague, or only about the mistake tends to create defensiveness instead of change.

  • Active listening, asking clarifying questions before reacting, usually reveals whether this is a skill gap, a capacity problem, or something else entirely.
  • Professionalism means addressing the pattern directly and privately, not letting teammates absorb it silently.
  • Effective feedback separates the behavior ('deadline missed three times') from the person, and ends with a clear, mutual next step.
Example

"You keep missing deadlines" invites defensiveness. "I've noticed the review has slipped three times this month, walk me through what's happening" invites information.

2

Think it through

  • What do you actually know versus what are you assuming about why the deadlines are being missed?
  • Who else is being affected by this pattern, and do they know it's being addressed?
  • What's the risk of waiting another cycle to say something?
  • What's the risk of addressing it publicly instead of privately?

Leading Through Change

You're rolling out a new CRM tagging system that will change how every agent logs a lead. Two senior agents have already told your team, informally, that the 'old way worked fine' and they don't plan to use the new tags.

1

The concept

Change Management is the structured approach to implementing operational change while minimizing disruption and helping people adapt. Resistance is rarely about the tool itself, it's usually about not understanding the reason for the change, or not being involved in it.

  • Explain the reason for the change before explaining the mechanics of the change.
  • Involve the people most affected, especially the resistant ones, rather than announcing at them.
  • Monitor adoption and gather feedback instead of assuming a launch email is the end of the rollout.
Example

Senior agents often resist not because they dislike new tools, but because a change was decided without asking what problem it solves for them specifically.

2

Think it through

  • What problem does the new tagging system actually solve, and have the two resistant agents heard that reason yet?
  • What's driving the resistance, habit, workload, or a legitimate concern about the new system?
  • What happens to your data and reporting if two senior agents simply don't adopt it?
  • Who has enough credibility with those two agents to help, if not you?

Active Listening Under Pressure

A teammate corners you between meetings, visibly frustrated, saying the new task-assignment process "makes no sense and is wasting everyone's time." You have four minutes before your next call.

1

The concept

Active listening means listening to understand, asking clarifying questions and confirming what you heard before reacting, not just waiting for your turn to respond. Under time pressure, the instinct is to solve or defend immediately; both usually shortcut the part where you actually understand the complaint.

  • Reflecting back what you heard ("so the extra approval step is what's slowing you down?") confirms understanding and often de-escalates on its own.
  • Defending the process before understanding the complaint signals you're not actually listening, even if your defense is correct.
  • Four minutes is enough to listen and schedule a real conversation, it's rarely enough to solve the actual problem.
Example

"That's not what it's for" (defending) versus "tell me what part is costing you the most time" (listening), the second gets you the real complaint in the same four minutes.

2

Think it through

  • What is the teammate actually frustrated about, the process itself, or something specific within it?
  • What would defending the process right now cost you, even if you're right?
  • Is four minutes enough to solve this, or only enough to understand it and set up a real conversation?
  • What would reflecting their concern back sound like, in your own words?

Navigating a Difficult Conversation

You need to tell a well-liked team member that a project they led, one they were proud of, didn't meet the client's expectations, and a portion of it needs to be redone.

1

The concept

Difficult conversations are hard because they usually involve both a factual disagreement (what happened) and an emotional stake (how it's received). Preparing for both, not just rehearsing the facts, is what separates a conversation that lands well from one that damages trust.

  • Lead with the specific, observable gap ('the client expected X, we delivered Y') rather than a general judgment ('this didn't work').
  • Acknowledge effort and ownership honestly before moving to what needs to change, skipping this reads as indifference to the work that was actually put in.
  • End with a clear, forward-looking next step, so the conversation closes on action rather than just the critique.
Example

"This isn't what I expected from you" is a judgment about the person. "The client expected X, and we delivered Y, let's figure out what closes that gap" is a fact about the work, paired with a next step.

2

Think it through

  • What is the specific, factual gap between what was expected and what was delivered?
  • What effort or strengths in this project are genuinely worth acknowledging before addressing the gap?
  • How might this land emotionally, given how proud they were of the work, and how does that change your opening line?
  • What's the concrete next step you want to leave them with?

Resolving Conflict Between Peers

Two team leads on your team have stopped speaking directly to each other and are routing every disagreement through you instead. Deadlines are starting to slip because decisions wait for you to relay messages.

1

The concept

Conflict resolution isn't about deciding who's right, it's about restoring a working relationship so decisions can happen without a translator. Becoming the permanent go-between might feel like helping, but it actually removes the incentive for either side to resolve things directly.

  • Being the relay solves the immediate problem (a decision gets made) but reinforces the underlying one (they still can't talk to each other).
  • A structured conversation, both sides present, focused on the specific decision at hand rather than the history of tension, is more effective than mediating each disagreement separately.
  • Your role is to facilitate a direct conversation, not to become the permanent translator between two people who report to you.
Example

Instead of relaying "she thinks the timeline is unrealistic" back and forth, bring both leads into one room and ask each to state their concern about the timeline directly, to each other.

2

Think it through

  • What's the cost of continuing to act as the go-between versus the short-term discomfort of a direct conversation?
  • Is there a specific decision blocking progress right now that needs to be resolved first?
  • What would each person need to hear from the other to move forward, even if they don't fully agree?
  • What's your role in this conversation, decision-maker, or facilitator?

Delegating With Ownership, Not Just Tasks

You've been personally handling weekly client check-in calls because "it's faster if I just do it." Your calendar is now the bottleneck for scheduling them, and you have three other priorities this week that keep slipping.

1

The concept

Delegation isn't just handing off a task, it's transferring ownership, including the judgment calls that come with it. Delegating a task without the context or authority to make decisions just moves the bottleneck to whoever you delegated to, and they'll be back at your desk for every call anyway.

  • Delegate the outcome (client feels consistently informed) not just the activity (make the call), that's what gives the other person room to use judgment.
  • "It's faster if I just do it" is often true in the short term and false in the long term, it keeps you as a permanent bottleneck on anything you delegate that way.
  • Clear success criteria and check-in points let you delegate real ownership without losing visibility.
Example

Delegating "make the weekly call" produces someone reading a script. Delegating "keep this client feeling consistently informed and flag anything that needs my attention" produces someone who can actually own the relationship.

2

Think it through

  • What decisions does this task actually require, and could someone else reasonably make them with the right context?
  • What's the real reason you've kept doing it yourself, speed, trust, or habit?
  • What would 'success' look like for whoever you delegate this to, in their own words?
  • What check-in cadence would let you delegate this without losing visibility into the client relationship?

Addressing Underperformance Early

A newer team member's work has been consistently below the bar for six weeks, not dramatically, but noticeably. No one has said anything directly yet, hoping they'll "find their footing."

1

The concept

Underperformance addressed early is a conversation. Underperformance addressed late is a pattern that's hard to untangle from morale, trust, and sunk time. Waiting for someone to "find their footing" without naming the gap removes their chance to actually close it.

  • Silence isn't kindness, it withholds the specific information someone needs to improve, and lets a fixable gap become a bigger one.
  • Address the pattern with specifics (which deliverables, what the bar looks like) rather than a vague sense that something's off.
  • Pair the conversation with support, training, clearer expectations, a check-in cadence, not just a warning.
Example

"Things haven't been quite right lately" gives someone nothing to act on. "The last three deliverables missed X and Y, here's what meeting the bar looks like, and let's check in weekly" gives them something to actually close.

2

Think it through

  • What specifically has been below the bar, can you point to concrete examples?
  • What might be causing the gap, skill, unclear expectations, or something else?
  • What's the cost of waiting another six weeks for them to 'find their footing' on their own?
  • What support, beyond naming the gap, would actually help them close it?

Negotiating a Deadline

A Sales Manager requests an immediate process change to how listings get approved. Your Operations team is already at capacity finishing a major client onboarding, and implementing the change this week would put that onboarding at risk.

1

The concept

Negotiation in Operations is rarely about winning, it's about finding the option that protects the business outcome both sides actually care about. The first step is separating the Sales Manager's position ('I need this now') from their underlying interest (faster listing approvals, fewer stalled deals).

  • Position = what someone asks for. Interest = the real problem behind the ask. Negotiate the interest, not the position.
  • Naming the trade-off out loud ('if we do this now, the onboarding slips') turns a conflict into a shared decision.
  • A good negotiated outcome usually isn't 'yes' or 'no', it's a modified scope, timeline, or sequence that protects both priorities.
Example

Instead of "we can't do this now," try: "here's what happens to the onboarding if we start this today, what if we start Thursday and pilot with one team first?"

2

Think it through

  • What is the Sales Manager's underlying interest, beyond the literal request?
  • What happens to the client onboarding if the team drops it to work on this instead?
  • Is there a smaller version of this change that could start now without the same risk?
  • Who else needs to be part of this conversation before a commitment is made?

Saying No Professionally

A long-standing vendor asks for an exception to your standard invoicing process, they want to skip the approval step 'just this once' because they're short-staffed. This is the third time this quarter they've asked for an exception.

1

The concept

Saying no professionally protects the process without damaging the relationship. The goal is to decline the exception while acknowledging the person, and to find common ground when possible, a version of 'yes' that doesn't compromise the standard.

  • State the reason the process exists, briefly, this isn't personal, it's structural.
  • Acknowledge the vendor's real constraint before declining; don't ignore it.
  • Offer what you can do, not just what you can't, this is where common ground usually lives.
Example

  • Is this a one-time constraint or a pattern that needs a different conversation?
  • What could you offer instead of the exception that still meets their real need (speed)?
  • What's the cost of granting this exception a third time, precedent-wise?

Managing Scope Creep

A client project originally scoped as "update the onboarding checklist" has, through a series of small requests, grown to include a new automation, two additional templates, and a training video, none of which were in the original agreement.

1

The concept

Scope creep rarely arrives as one big ask, it accumulates through small, individually reasonable requests. Managing it isn't about refusing every addition; it's about making each addition visible as a choice, with its own cost, instead of letting it absorb silently into the original scope.

  • Each small addition may be reasonable on its own, the problem is that none of them were evaluated against the original scope or timeline.
  • Naming the delta ('this project has grown from X to X+3') isn't confrontational, it's information the client needs to make an informed choice.
  • Offer the addition as a choice: extend the timeline, adjust the fee, or hold it for a phase two, rather than silently absorbing it or refusing outright.
Example

"Sure, I can add that" three times in a row quietly triples the scope with no corresponding change to timeline or cost, until the original deadline arrives and nothing fits.

2

Think it through

  • How much has the scope actually grown from the original agreement, item by item?
  • What's driving the additions, genuine new needs, or scope that should have been in the original discussion?
  • What are the real options: extend the timeline, adjust the cost, or defer some items to a later phase?
  • How do you raise this without sounding like you're penalizing the client for asking questions?

Escalation vs. Negotiation

A vendor has missed a second deadline with a vague explanation. You could push back and negotiate a new timeline yourself, or escalate to your manager to apply more pressure. You're not sure which the situation actually calls for.

1

The concept

Escalation and negotiation solve different problems. Negotiation is for situations where a mutually workable solution likely exists and you have the standing to reach it. Escalation is for situations involving a pattern, a risk beyond your authority, or a relationship that needs more leverage than you currently hold.

  • Ask what's actually missing before choosing: is it a solution (negotiate) or leverage/authority (escalate)?
  • Escalating too early on something negotiable signals you can't resolve issues at your level. Negotiating too long on something that needs escalation lets risk compound.
  • A second missed deadline with a vague explanation is often the threshold where a pattern, not a one-off, starts to justify escalation, but try direct negotiation with a clear ask first.
Example

One missed deadline with a real reason: negotiate a new date directly. A second miss with a vague excuse: negotiate once more with a clear, firm ask, and prepare to escalate if it happens again.

2

Think it through

  • Is this the first miss or a pattern, and does that change what's needed?
  • Do you have the standing and information to negotiate a firm new commitment yourself?
  • What would escalating now accomplish that negotiating directly wouldn't?
  • What's the cost of escalating too early versus negotiating too long?

Aligning Stakeholders on a Contested Decision

You need to decide whether to switch CRMs. Sales wants to keep the current system (they know it well); Marketing wants a switch (better automation); leadership wants a decision by Friday and no more back-and-forth.

1

1

The concept

Most processes were designed to solve a specific problem at a specific time, and once that context is forgotten, the process becomes tradition rather than a deliberate choice. First-principles thinking means asking what this process would look like if you designed it today, from the actual objective, rather than assuming the current version is necessarily correct just because it's established.

  • What is the principle? Question whether a process reflects a deliberate choice or an inherited habit nobody has re-examined.
  • Why does it matter? Processes that go unexamined accumulate steps that no longer serve their original purpose, slowing the business without anyone noticing.

  • Effective behavior: asking what the process is actually trying to protect against, then checking whether the current steps are the most direct way to protect it.
  • Common mistake: assuming that because something has worked, it's still the best version of itself.

1

The concept

There's a real difference between making an existing process modestly better and asking whether the process should exist in its current form at all.   • What is the principle? Distinguish between improving a process and questioning whether it's still the right process.

  • Effective behavior: evaluating the automation option honestly against the cost of continuing incremental work, even if it means the past two quarters' effort gets partially superseded.
  • Common mistake: sunk-cost thinking, continuing down the incremental path because of the effort already invested in it.

Building Trust Before Expecting Healthy Conflict

In team meetings, disagreements rarely surface out loud, people nod along, then you hear the real objections afterward, one-on-one, in the hallway.

1

The concept
